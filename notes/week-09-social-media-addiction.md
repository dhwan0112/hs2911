## 1. One-paragraph summary

Internet addiction (problematic internet use, PIU) is framed as an impulse control disorder: dependence on the internet for psychological wellbeing; social media addiction is a subset focused on habitual problematic use of social media platforms [S2]. "Problematic" use (preoccupation, lack of control) is distinct from but correlated with "intense" use (duration, frequency) [S3]. Problematic use is operationalised through DSM-5 Internet Gaming Disorder criteria (the only internet addiction in DSM-5) and linked to a dopamine-driven loop and weakened top-down control in the brain [S3, S4]. People use social media for three functions (social interactions, community inputs, content sharing) and two motivations (belongingness, self-presentation) [S5]; four models (cognitive-behavioural, social skill, social cognitive, theory of planned behaviour) explain addiction [S6]. Reasons are examined from the user perspective (life satisfaction, personality, demographics) [S7] and the platform perspective (anonymity, algorithm, surveillance, never-ending content, asynchronous communication) [S8], and they feed a vicious cycle [S9]. Effects span cognitive, physical, mental-health, risk-behaviour and body-image domains [S10]. Solutions are individual (self-intervention, behavioural therapy) plus societal (platform regulation, public education, reduced reliance); research is mostly on students aged 19–25 and ignores platform differences [S14].

## 2. Key concepts and definitions

- **Internet addiction / PIU** — impulse control disorder; "dependence on the internet for psychological wellbeing", deprivation produces heightened unpleasant feelings. Longstreet & Brooks (2017). `[S2]`
- **Social media addiction** — a SUBSET of internet addiction focused on habitual problematic use of social media platforms. Not the reverse, not interchangeable, not a replacement diagnosis. `[S2]`
- **Problematic use** — preoccupation and lack of control; behavioural symptoms; "driven by an uncontrollable motivation to log on... impairs other important life areas" (Pellegrino et al., 2022). `[S3]`
- **Intense use** — duration and frequency. Intense vs problematic = "distinct but correlated concepts" (Boer et al., 2020). Hours online alone do NOT define problematic use. `[S3]`
- **DSM-5 / IGD** — Internet Gaming Disorder is the ONLY form of internet addiction currently in DSM-5 (5th revision); its criteria are used as the diagnostic reference (Besser et al., 2019). Social media addiction is not a formal DSM-5 diagnosis. `[S3]`
- **Biological comparison** — internet addiction is associated with impairments in brain regions for reward, motivation and control, "with both similarities and differences compared to the effects of addictive drugs" (Park et al., 2016). Not "identical" to drugs. `[S3]`
- **Dopamine-driven feedback loop** — social media usage results in this loop. `[S3]`
- **Reward circuit** — dopamine pathway VTA -> nucleus accumbens (ventral striatum) -> ventral pallidum; responds to rewards and guides behaviour toward them. Hippocampus = memories of use; amygdala = emotional aspects; ACG + PFC = cognitive control ("top-down", glutamate/GABA balance); OFC = value/salience and decision to use, final say = "NO-GO". `[S4]`
- **Addicted brain** — motivation stronger, memories more dominant, top-down mechanisms weaker -> "GO" state. `[S4]`
- **Functions vs motivations** — functions (what it does): social interactions, community inputs, content sharing. Motivations (why users go): belongingness, self-presentation. Do not mix lists. `[S5]`
- **Belongingness** — seeking acceptance; communicating to care. `[S5]`
- **Self-presentation** — seeking attention; self-disclosure to express. (Seidman, 2013) `[S5]`
- **Life satisfaction chain** — more happiness -> higher life satisfaction -> LOWER addiction; more stress -> lower life satisfaction -> HIGHER addiction; associated with depression, loneliness, trauma (Longstreet & Brooks, 2017). `[S7]`
- **Vicious cycle** — convenience of online connectivity -> temporary surrogate for happiness -> long-lasting addiction of stress -> disruption of offline relationships (-> back). Slide poses "root cause?" and "where to intervene?" as open questions. `[S9]`
- **FoMO** — constant bombardment of virtual updates (fear of missing out); listed as an effect of addiction [S10] and studied on [S11, S12].
- **SNUD** (social networks use disorder) — tendency construct in Montag & Markett; mediates FoMO -> cognitive failures among users; measured with the Social Networking Sites-Addiction Test (SNS-AT). `[S12]`
- **Upward social comparison** — exposure to unrealistic portrayals; exacerbates low self-esteem and body-image dissatisfaction. `[S10]`
- **Responsibility of society** — systemic regulation of platform access, public education, reduced reliance on internet usage to improve public welfare. `[S14]`

## 3. Frameworks, models and typologies

**DSM-5 IGD criteria as listed on slide (9 items)** `[S3]` (Besser et al., 2019):
1. Preoccupation  2. Withdrawal  3. Tolerance  4. Deception  5. Unsuccessful attempts to stop/reduce  6. Loss of interest in other hobbies  7. Excessive use despite problems  8. Escape from negative mood  9. Jeopardized relationships/opportunities

**Intense vs problematic** `[S3]`: Intense = duration and frequency. Problematic = preoccupation and lack of control.

**Three functions** `[S5]` (Pellegrino et al., 2022):
1. Social interactions — globalised interconnectedness; post-pandemic significance
2. Community inputs — freedom to voice opinions; networking and collaborating
3. Content sharing — form of entertainment; form of education

**Two motivations** `[S5]` (Seidman, 2013):
1. Belongingness — seeking acceptance; communicating to care
2. Self-presentation — seeking attention; self-disclosure to express

Note: different platforms have different purposes and user behaviours `[S5]`.

**Four models of social media addiction** (Ho et al., 2017) `[S6]`

| Model | Focus | View of what social media does |
|---|---|---|
| Cognitive-behavioural | maladaptation in cognitive processes | relief from psychosocial issues |
| Social skill | deficits in social interaction | substitute for offline communication |
| Social cognitive | deficiency in self-regulation | mindless habit of consumption |
| Theory of planned behaviour | inclination in subjective attitude and norms | behaviour aligned with self-interest and societal approval |

(Model-to-view pairing is confirmed by Q10 rationale.)

**Reasons for addiction — user perspective** `[S7]`
- Emotional states and life satisfaction (chain above).
- Personality traits (Pellegrino et al., 2022): Extraversion = easier communication and relationship-building online; Introversion = the slide text repeats the same phrase, "easier communication and relationship-building online" (this is what the slide literally says, likely a slide typo; be ready for either trait); Narcissism = desire to be "seen" and "liked"; Neuroticism = desire to express self, feel belonging and be in control.
- Demographics (Pellegrino et al., 2022): Female > Male; Single > Attached; Older (adolescence) > Younger (childhood).
- Slide 7 also maps motivations and models onto reasons (belongingness, self-presentation; four models).

**Reasons for addiction — platform perspective** `[S8]` (Pellegrino et al., 2022; Ho et al., 2017)
1. Potential for anonymity — safe space to comment
2. Algorithm of recommendations — catered to personalised user engagement
3. Normalisation of surveillance — ability to see what others are doing online
4. Never-ending content — automaticity of scrolling
5. Asynchronous communication — take time to craft messages and present best self

**Vicious cycle** `[S9]`: convenience of online connectivity -> temporary surrogate for happiness -> long-lasting addiction of stress -> disruption of offline relationships.

**Six effects of social media addiction** `[S10]`
1. Greater potential for general risks of improper internet use (sexting, stalking, cyber-bullying, privacy breaches)
2. Reduced performance on cognitive tasks and mental effort (academic outcomes, school wellbeing)
3. Constant bombardment of virtual updates (FoMO) + comorbidity of depression, anxiety, stress
4. Displacement of routine lifestyle patterns (exercise, eating, sleep -> obesity, carpal tunnel syndrome, etc.)
5. Excessive exposure to unrealistic portrayals (upward social comparison -> low self-esteem, body image dissatisfaction)
6. Enhanced tendencies for sensation seeking (drugs, alcoholism, unsafe sex)

**Solutions and challenges** `[S14]`
- Treatment for the individual: self-intervention methods; behavioural therapies (Ji et al., 2023).
- Responsibility of society: systemic regulation of platform access; public education; reduced reliance on internet usage to improve public welfare (Pellegrino et al., 2022; Longstreet & Brooks, 2017).
- Further research: current research mainly on students aged 19–25 -> study other age groups; platforms differ in purpose/behaviour -> reasons may differ by platform.

## 6. Arguments, critiques and open questions

- Intensity is not pathology: duration/frequency and preoccupation/lack of control are correlated but separate, so hours alone are a poor measure of problematic use `[S3]`.
- Social media addiction has no formal DSM-5 status; IGD criteria are borrowed as a reference `[S3]`.
- Brain-drug analogy is partial: similarities AND differences `[S3]`.
- Platform differences: each platform has different purposes and user behaviours, so reasons may differ `[S5, S14]`.
- Platform-side design (never-ending feed, recommendations) vs user-side motives (belongingness, self-presentation): removing features changes automaticity but not motives (quiz inference) `[S5, S8]`.
- Vicious cycle: root cause and appropriate intervention point are left as open questions; strengthening offline wellbeing is a reasoned inference, not a stated slide answer `[S9]`.
- Prevalence figures are limited: student-only 2017 sample cannot be generalised to all young people or to the present `[S2]`.
- Correlation vs causation: Montag & Markett cross-sectional (cannot establish direction); ADHD headline overstates `[S12]`.
- Individual vs society: treatment alone is incomplete; regulation of access, public education and reduced reliance are also needed `[S14]`.
- Sampling gap: most studies are on students 19–25; need other age groups and platform comparisons `[S14]`.
- Demographic associations (female, single, adolescent) may not generalise `[S7, S14]`.

## 7. Distractor traps

- **Reversed subset**: internet addiction as subset of social media addiction, or SM addiction "replaced" internet addiction (Q2).
- **Swapped definitions**: intense vs problematic definitions flipped, or "unrelated" (Q3); "hours per day" definitions.
- **Overstated DSM status**: social media addiction or all internet addiction as formal DSM-5 diagnoses (Q4).
- **List mixing**: functions and motivations swapped or blended (Q5); model foci shuffled (Q6, Q10).
- **Reversed chain**: more happiness lowering life satisfaction, or addiction -> stress direction (Q7, Q24, Q33).
- **Number out of context**: treating 29.5% as share of the 84.7% (Q8), or extrapolating 2017 students to all young people today (Q19).
- **Absolute claims**: "identical to drugs", "unique to social media", "ends all addiction", "only cause" (Q22, Q38).
- **Correlation as causation**: ADHD, FoMO -> cognitive failures (Q20, Q21, Q12). Also "cross-sectional is a strength / rules out reverse causation".
- **Feature-description mismatch**: correct description attached to the wrong platform feature (Q11, Q25).
- **Right concept, wrong model**: relief from psychosocial issues = cognitive-behavioural, not social cognitive (Q13); mindless habit = social cognitive (Q14).
- **Right finding, wrong study**: Doan vs Dam; Doan's popular platforms were Facebook, Zalo, YouTube, NOT Instagram/TikTok (Q26).
- **Partial solution**: treatment-only, regulation-only, ban-based or research-delay plans (Q23, Q30).
- **Wrong construct in design questions**: measures of intensity (hours, followers) used for criteria (Q35, Q40); cross-sectional designs for direction (Q34).
- **Overgeneralising**: students 19–25 findings applied to all ages/platforms (Q27, Q31).
