## 1. One-paragraph summary

Cyberbullying is defined (Tokunaga, 2010) by intentionality, repetition and power imbalance, and it is a "no safe place" problem because the medium follows victims anywhere, any hour [S6]. It is common (about 40% of adults globally, about 50% of adolescents in SG/CN/MY/KR) yet under-reported in Singapore, where official figures are "low and stable" [S7]. Antecedents combine individual motives (Runions/Howard: rage, revenge, reward, recreation) with ICT affordances (anonymity, cue paucity, permanence, perpetual audience, temporal lag) and platform design (Kramer et al. emotional contagion; SIDE group-norm conformity) [S9-S11]. Consequences include depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, physical and academic harm; bully-victims fare worst and are missed by standard frameworks [S13]. Billington et al. (2026) use recognition theory on Netflix's Adolescence to show a real-but-not-inevitable pathway from victimisation to misrecognition to algorithmic extremism to harmful recognition to escalation [S17].

## 2. Key concepts and definitions

- **Cyberbullying (Tokunaga synthesised definition)** — "Any behaviour performed through electronic or digital media by individuals or groups that repeatedly communicates hostile or aggressive messages intended to inflict harm or discomfort on others." [S6]
- **Intentionality** — aggressor intends harm; accidental acts do not count (matters for Runions' typology) [S6].
- **Repetition** — single incidents may not qualify; but digital permanence complicates this: "one post, seen 1000 times" [S6].
- **Power imbalance** — technical skill, anonymity and audience scale create asymmetries that differ from offline bullying [S6].
- **"No safe place"** — not rhetoric but a structural feature of the medium: cyberbullying can follow victims into any space, at any hour [S6].
- **Not all online aggression is cyberbullying** — Tokunaga's three criteria filter it [S6].
- **Dark figure problem** — official/reported cases (MOE: "low and stable") understate reality; research consistently shows under-reporting; 43% of Singaporeans who experienced online harm would not act because they do not believe it will help [S7].
- **Bully-victim overlap** — those who both perpetrate and experience cyberbullying; 4.8% of SG adolescents [S7, S13].
- **Bully-victim paradox** — bully-victims report WORSE outcomes (depression, anxiety, physical symptoms) than pure victims or pure perpetrators (Kowalski & Limber); standard victim-support or perpetrator-discipline frameworks miss them [S13].
- **Howard's Quadripartite Violence Typology (via Runions, 2013)** — four motives on two axes (impulsive/controlled x reactive/appetitive) [S9].
- **Rage** — impulsive-reactive; provocation, anger, immediate lashing out; fatigue and sleep deprivation amplify ("the internet never sleeps") [S9].
- **Revenge** — controlled-reactive; perceived injustice + self-control = planned retaliation; permanence of insults fuels rumination [S9].
- **Reward** — controlled-appetitive; instrumental aggression for social status or material gain; anonymity lowers perceived cost [S9].
- **Recreation** — impulsive-appetitive; aggression as entertainment, thrill-seeking, reduced empathy online; "it was just a joke" [S9].
- **ICT affordances (Runions Fig. 2)** — anonymity, cue paucity, permanence, perpetual audience, temporal lag [S10] (see section 3).
- **Hostile attribution bias** — amplified by cue paucity (no nonverbal feedback) [S10].
- **Emotional contagion (Kramer, Guillory & Hancock, 2014)** — platform architecture shapes emotional state; negative-curated feed yields more negative expression [S11].
- **Platforms are not neutral pipes** — engagement-maximising algorithms can amplify emotional conditions preceding aggression without any individual choosing aggression [S11].
- **SIDE model (Reicher, Spears & Postmes, 1995; wk 2/3 callback)** — anonymity does not simply disinhibit; it makes group identity more salient; anonymous actors hyper-conform to ingroup norms when the visible identity is the ingroup [S11].
- **Moral disengagement vs SIDE** — moral disengagement = HOW individuals justify crossing lines; SIDE = WHERE justifications come from and why they feel collectively sanctioned. Together: the platform renders ingroup aggression normative before any individual decision [S11].
- **Recognition theory (Honneth, 1995; via Billington et al.)** — asks whose suffering from cyberbullying is recognised as legitimate harm; "Recognition from harmful communities is still recognition." [S17, S27]
- **Misrecognition** — adults/institutions fail to acknowledge or respond to the victim's harm; step 2 of the pathway [S17].
- **Adolescence (Netflix, 2025)** — dramatisation (120M viewers): Jamie, 13, cyberbullying on Instagram -> humiliation -> incel radicalisation -> lethal violence; treated as a Rorschach test for moral panic [S4].

## 3. Frameworks, models and typologies

**Tokunaga's three criteria** [S6]: (1) Intentionality, (2) Repetition, (3) Power imbalance.

**Howard / Runions Quadripartite Violence Typology** [S9] (all four, in slide order) and slide-implied intervention:

| Motive | Type | Core | Intervention [S9] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rage | Impulsive-Reactive | Provocation -> anger -> immediate lashing out; fatigue amplifies | De-escalation |
| Revenge | Controlled-Reactive | Perceived injustice + self-control = planned retaliation; permanence fuels rumination | (not stated on slide) |
| Reward | Controlled-Appetitive | Instrumental, status/material gain; anonymity lowers cost | Structural change |
| Recreation | Impulsive-Appetitive | Entertainment, thrill, reduced empathy; "just a joke" | Empathy training |

Slide moral: interventions that treat "cyberbullies" as one group will fail [S9].

**Runions ICT affordances -> effect (Fig. 2)** [S10]:
1. Anonymity — lowers perceived cost; decouples identity from act.
2. Cue paucity — text-only, no nonverbal feedback; hostile attribution bias amplified.
3. Permanence — screenshots live forever; rumination prolonged.
4. Perpetual audience — peers always watching; thrill amplified.
5. Temporal lag — time gap enables planned revenge ("dish best served cold").

**Linking SIDE + Runions** [S11]: Anonymity = lower perceived cost (Runions) + ingroup salience (SIDE) -> aggression feels sanctioned.

**Consequences taxonomy (Kowalski & Limber)** [S13]:
- Mental health: depression, anxiety, loneliness; lower self-esteem; suicidal ideation (more strongly linked than offline bullying).
- Physical: headaches, sleep disturbance; psychosomatic symptoms; increased absenteeism.
- Academic: disengagement from school; lower grades; avoidance of online learning tools.

**Radicalisation pathway (Billington et al.)** [S17], 5 steps in order:
1. Cyberbullying victimisation — repeated public humiliation -> shame and identity threat.
2. Misrecognition — adults/institutions fail to acknowledge or respond.
3. Platform algorithm — grievance + absence of recognition -> recommended extremist content.
4. Harmful recognition — incel/manosphere community gives belonging and validation.
5. Escalation — ideology normalises violence as legitimate response.
Caveat: real but not inevitable; never excuses harm perpetrated. Key tension: the show's framing risks obscuring gendered harm to victim Katie.

## 6. Arguments, critiques and open questions

- Single incident vs repetition: permanence means one post can act repeatedly, but this only "complicates" and does not automatically satisfy repetition [S6].
- Definition is narrower than "all online aggression"; intent excludes accidents [S6].
- Official "low and stable" figures vs dark figure of under-reporting; low trust that reporting helps (43%) [S7].
- Not only an adolescent problem or a UK problem: SG adults and adolescents affected [S3].
- Motive heterogeneity: one-size interventions fail; match response to motive [S9].
- Platforms vs individuals: platforms are not neutral pipes; the platform renders ingroup aggression normative before individual decision [S11]. Note SIDE counters the simple "anonymity = disinhibition" view [S11].
- Adolescence is a dramatisation: pathway real but not inevitable; never excuses harm; risk of making victim Katie's death incidental; moral panic [S4, S17].
- Recognition from harmful communities is still recognition: misrecognition by adults opens the door [S17].
- Limitations of evidence to note: Kramer was done without consent (ethics) [S11].

## 7. Distractor traps

- **Absolute / over-strong claim** — "inevitable", "proves", "any", "always", "little effect in any context" (Q21C, Q19B, Q23D, Q22C). Slides use "real but not inevitable", "necessary but not sufficient".
- **Number out of context / mismatched pairing** — 84% (harmful content) vs ~50% (cyberbullying); 49% (parents' concern) vs 84%; adults 40% vs adolescents 50% swapped (Q7).
- **Reversed direction/swapped labels** — affective vs cognitive empathy swapped; offline/online swapped (Q11, Q34, Q30).
- **Motive-intervention mismatch** — rage with structural change; recreation with de-escalation; reward with de-escalation (Q9, Q15, Q29).
- **ICT property-effect mismatch** — each property paired with another's effect (Q10: temporal lag/permanence/anonymity swapped).
- **Wrong construct** — treating SIDE as simple disinhibition or as reduced conformity (Q5, Q22, Q24); confusing cue paucity with giving more information (Q24C).
- **Rank/sequence scrambles** — pathway steps reordered or "supportive content / recovery" endings (Q26).
- **Right idea, wrong group** — bully-victims described as protected, better off, or same as perpetrators (Q17, Q25, Q40); Q4 distractors describe one-off retaliation or bystanders.
- **Over-correction in plans** — treating bully-victims as only victims or only perpetrators (Q32); both/together is correct.
- **Reasoning from low reports to low prevalence** — "low and stable so little need" (Q20E, Q20D); correct answer cites under-reporting.
- **Individualising the burden** — answers putting responsibility on perpetrators, bystanders, or "report and wait" instead of structural measures (Q27, Q35, Q31).
- **Age/gender distractors** — age and gender are not significant once cognition/affect controlled (Q16D, Q30E, Q34C/D).
- **Invented causal claims** — "based on research that most cyberbullied boys become violent" (Q21E).
- **Plausible-sounding detail not on slides** — e.g., physical-threat or school-setting requirements in the definition (Q1B, Q19D); anything absent from slides is wrong.

## 9. 60-second recap

- Cyberbullying = intentionality + repetition + power imbalance (Tokunaga); permanence complicates repetition; "no safe place" is structural [S6].
- ~40% adults globally; ~50% adolescents in SG/CN/MY/KR; 84% = harmful content (not cyberbullying); 49% parents' top concern; 43% would not act [S7].
- Official "low and stable" = dark figure of under-reporting [S7].
- Four motives: rage (impulsive-reactive, de-escalation), revenge (controlled-reactive), reward (controlled-appetitive, structural change), recreation (impulsive-appetitive, empathy training) [S9].
