## 1. One-paragraph summary
Social media promised that anyone could speak, be seen and belong, but visibility was never neutral and belonging came at a cost [P1-S3]. The guiding question is "what does it cost to be seen on platforms that were not built for you?" [P1-S4]. Three communities show three strategies: Black Americans on Twitter signify (language as racial identity when the body is invisible; cost = meaning leaks), queer women on Instagram do microcelebrity/identity labour (cost = harassment, self-censorship, moderation), Palestinians on Instagram practise countervisuality (images of home/memory/place as territorial claim; cost = algorithmic suppression, removal, epistemic erasure) [P1-S5]. Common threads: identity must be actively constructed; platforms are not neutral; collective performance is the strategy; visibility and vulnerability are the same thing [P1-S22]. The mental-health thread: collective protection, cost of harassment, platform as adversary, solidarity as resilience [P1-S23]. Part 2 details the Palestinian study: 9,126 posts from 834 users, analysed for themes and compared with March 2024 press coverage of 5 cities; Instagram foregrounds everyday life, mobilisation and resilience while news foregrounds knowledge framing, death and destruction [P2-S13, S17, S30]. Countervisuality is expressed through three goals: claiming space, coding resistance, collective remembering [P2-S19].

## 2. Key concepts and definitions

### Part 1 — framing
- **The promise of social media** — anyone could speak (no gatekeepers/editors/licence), be seen (audiences assemble around interest, not capital), belong (community beyond geography: diaspora, minority, fringe). Rebuttal: "visibility was never neutral — and belonging always came at a cost" [P1-S3].
- **Signal words** — warm-up activity: in-group markers (e.g. Singlish "bopian", "sian sia", keyboard smash, custom stickers, "55555"); used when chat register shifts to mark safety/intimacy, never with parents/boss, "only when I stop monitoring myself". Link to Q40: asking students when/with whom they use signals applies "identity must be performed" + "in-group language needs cultural competence" [P1-S6].
- **Platforms not built for you** — the lecture's driving question [P1-S4].

### Part 1 — Florini (2014), Black Twitter
- **Corporeal signifiers (offline vs online)** — Offline, race is tied to skin, hair, features; the body does communicative work automatically, identity perceived without effort. Online, body can be obscured or imitated (deceptive avatar, anonymous handle); identity must be actively performed [P1-S8]. Contrast trap: Q2 reverses these.
- **"Blackness is a practice — it is something you do"** — Baratunde Thurston (SXSW 2010), cited in Florini [P1-S8].
- **Signifyin'** — genre of linguistic performance rooted in Black American oral traditions: wordplay, indirectness, doubleness, misdirection; conveys multiple layers of meaning at once. Generally involves humour and wit; a powerful resource for signalling racial identity (Florini 2014, Television & New Media 15(3), 223-237) [P1-S7, S9].
- **Properties of signifyin' (four, after the genre definition)** [P1-S9]:
  - *Cultural competence required* — only those who already know can participate; outsiders hear the surface; meaning lives in shared context.
  - *Resistance through indirection* — during slavery and beyond, encoded critique of oppression; a joke carrying a second, political argument.
  - *Solidarity through performance* — collaborative (retweeting, adding, one-upping); community performs itself into being.
  - *Oral tradition made new* — hashtags, retweets, speed map onto call-and-response.
- **Meaning leaks / code leaks** — when trending topics pull signifyin' into mainstream visibility, outsiders lacking cultural competence may appropriate, mock or weaponise it. Same feature (public hashtags) creates solidarity and exposure. Cultural competence is "the entry fee" [P1-S5, S11].
- **"Pass" quote** — "In a medium where users could 'pass,' many Black users prioritise the performance of their racial identity" [P1-S11].
- **Language is identity infrastructure** — when the body cannot signal race online, language carries the load. Mental-health implication: belonging via collective linguistic performance is both protective and precarious; power depends on remaining partially opaque to outsiders [P1-S12].
- **Bonilla-Silva axis-of-division claim** — "In order for people to struggle along an axis of social division, that axis must be visible and real to them" (slide spells "Bonna-Silva"; speaker notes say Bonilla-Silva). Pushback against colourblindness: refusing to name race loses the ability to discuss racial inequality. Performing Blackness when one could pass = keeping the axis visible, a precondition for solidarity, not vanity [P1-S12 notes].

### Part 1 — Duguay (2017), queer women on Instagram
- **Ghost effect** — Castle (1993), cited in Duguay: queer women's invisibility stems from women's sexuality being conceivable only in relation to men's [P1-S15].
- **Media timeline** — see section 3 [P1-S15].
- **Microcelebrity / branded self** — gaining attention on Instagram requires a coherent branded self combining intimate disclosure with stylised visual performance. Hearn (cited in Duguay): labour of producing "a public persona that might be of practical or relational use" within unstable neoliberal economies [P1-S16 notes].
- **Reflectivity / Reflexivity / Refraction / Aspirational labour** — four ideas; see section 3. Contrast trap: reflexivity = feedback loop with audiences (copy popular accounts, adjust to follower response); refraction = reaching unintended audiences (family, harassers, moderators) [P1-S16].
- **"The more I look like Justin Bieber in the pictures, the better"** — Alex (@a1exthehuman). Lesbian culture appropriated Bieber's feminine masculinity as marker of queerness; masculine (asserts identity) and ironic (boyishness questioned); the "boi" aesthetic = soft masculine rather than butch. Celebrity culture repurposed as identity signal on a platform that rewards visual reference to fame [P1-S17].
- **Kelzz (@kelzz32)** — African American woman, western US, warehouse worker, hip-hop/urban dancer; wanted audience large enough to fund a dance tour; #lesbian partly identity declaration, partly to filter followers to her community; Instagram "broke the shell I was hiding in"; gained followers after a celebrity regrammed a dance video [P1-S17].
- **Mïta (@mitagibson)** — trans activist, married, baby son; #LGBTfamily to challenge idea that only a man and woman can make a family; answers every private message from trans people about transition; cross-posts to Reddit; #translesbian to filter toward similar users [P1-S17].
- **Costs of visibility** — sexual harassment, platform moderation, self-censorship, the invisible labour (section 3) [P1-S18].
- **Queenie** — quoted: straight male followers found her lesbian identity "a turn-off because they couldn't imagine things"; photo of two women in underwear flagged and removed (less exposed than comparable straight content); harassment rose after couple selfies; moved nude modelling to Tumblr [P1-S18].
- **Duguay takeaway** — "Visibility is aspirational labour — and the rewards are unequal." Platform architecture rewarded normative femininity, mainstream celebrity culture, heterosexual aesthetics. Mental-health costs are embedded in everyday practices, not separate from them [P1-S19].

### Part 2 — Mujtahid, Rashid & Jaidka (2025)
- **Settler-colonial logic of "elimination"** — two parallel erasures: *Physical reality / material erasure* (material destruction, land confiscation and displacement, settlement expansion, administrative fragmentation, restriction and surveillance) vs *Digital reality / symbolic erasure* (control of visibility, memory, narrative; social media censorship; news framing). Territorial erasure example: Arabic place names replaced, maps redrawn, depopulated villages overwritten by new national narratives [P2-S2].
- **Third place** — Ray Oldenburg, *The Great Good Place* (1989): third places matter for democracy, civic engagement, sense of place. Framing quote: "The digital can erase — but the very same mechanisms can also be reversed, reengineered, and reclaimed." [P2-S3]
- **Digital placemaking (Context)** — use of digital tools (maps, geotags, posts) to represent and organise space online; platforms let users tag, locate, share places; shapes how places are seen, remembered, understood. Creates a "floating homeland" through imagery and memory construction [P2-S5]. Described as non-confrontational yet potent response to algorithmic erasure and hate [P2-S7].
- **Symbolic geographies (Concept)** — places are not just physical; mental constructions made through images, narratives, representations; media/discourse shape who is seen as belonging or not belonging [P2-S6].
- **Countervisuality (Implication)** — Nicholas Mirzoeff, *The Right to Look* (2011): decolonial visual framework challenging dominant systems of seeing; resistance from below; right to see, be seen on one's own terms, refuse the imposed gaze of power; gives visibility to alternative narratives and maintains historical continuity via images. Instagram UGC reorients how Palestinian space and life are seen [P2-S7].
- **Research gaps** — most scholarship = textual analysis of mainstream press and political discourse, little on citizen-led visual journalism; limited theorisation of how repeated digital imagery acts as spatial/symbolic reconstruction to preserve memory and resist disappearance under occupation and networked censorship [P2-S8].
- **Three high-level communicative goals** — claiming space, coding resistance, collective remembering [P2-S19]. Details in section 3.
- **Possessive naming and geotagging** — assert belonging against cartographic erasure (#pieceofpalestine, #humansofjerusalem) [P2-S20, S24].
- **Strategic ambiguity** — symbolism/ambiguity; circumvents moderation via indirect or coded language; meanings need cultural or contextual knowledge [P2-S25]. Direct parallel to Florini's signifyin' (Q25).
- **Recurring motifs** — collective visual shorthand used to circumvent moderation where explicit political reference is censored [P2-S26].
- **Networked archive** — posts accumulate into a collective visual archive enabling collective remembering and preserving presence/memory against erasure [P2-S27, S29].
- **Conclusion claims** — platforms are not neutral (govern visibility); visual storytelling becomes strategy (symbolism, ambiguity, coded forms to navigate moderation); representation is not passive (claiming space, resisting erasure, asserting presence) [P2-S29].

## 3. Frameworks, models and typologies

### Part 1
**Three communities, three strategies, three costs** [P1-S5]
| # | Community | Strategy | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 01 | Black Americans on Twitter | Signifyin': language as racial identity when body invisible | Meaning leaks: in-group code goes viral, loses protection |
| 02 | Queer women on Instagram | Microcelebrity: selfies, hashtags, celebrity references as identity labour | Harassment, self-censorship, platform moderation of queer bodies |
| 03 | Palestinians on Instagram | Countervisuality: images of home, memory, place as territorial claim | Algorithmic suppression, content removal, epistemic erasure |

**Florini: three hashtag cases** [P1-S10]
1. #DrakePunchlines — *identity signal*; mocking Drake's lyrical style ("hashtag rap"); requires knowing hip hop; performs community membership through wit and intertextual reference.
2. #BlackNerdsUnite — *social critique*; challenges idea that Blackness = anti-intellectual; self-deprecating on surface, oppositional underneath (example tweet: "visit the library? shoot, I WORK at the library!").
3. #NextOnNightline — *political resistance*; mocks ABC's panel on why Black women "can't find a man"; intersectional critique of race + gender politics in media.

**Signifyin' properties** (in slide order) [P1-S9]: genre definition → cultural competence required → resistance through indirection → solidarity through performance → oral tradition made new.

**Duguay: queer women's media timeline** [P1-S15]
1. Pre-1990s — LGBTQ people largely absent from screen media; one-off episodes for dramatic effect then erased.
2. 1990s — "Lesbian chic": queer women shown as normatively feminine to retain male audiences; visible but on heterosexual terms.
3. 2000s–now — "Gaystreaming": white, upper-class LGBTQ characters for mainstream appeal; erases class, race, non-conformity.
4. Instagram — opportunity to represent without gatekeepers, but new/different constraints.

**Duguay: microcelebrity, four ideas** [P1-S16]
1. Reflectivity — platform mirrors a desired self; hashtags like #lesbian = personal declaration + community affiliation simultaneously.
2. Reflexivity — ongoing feedback loop with audiences; studied popular accounts, replicated techniques, adjusted to follower response.
3. Refraction — self-representations reach unintended audiences (family, harassers, moderators); constant management of who sees what. Quote: hard to communicate lesbian identity in a selfie without being with partner.
4. Aspirational labour — creative self-presentation promising social and economic capital; rewards uneven, tied to gendered expectations.

**Duguay: costs of visibility, four items** [P1-S18]
1. Sexual harassment — sexualising DMs, heterosexual fantasy persists from broadcast media; blocking/ignoring = daily unpaid labour.
2. Platform moderation — underwear photo of two women flagged/removed though less exposed than straight content; homophobic users "bash on my page".
3. Self-censorship — self-censored or relocated content to other platforms (Queenie: nude modelling to Tumblr); multiple accounts.
4. The invisible labour — managing harassment, audience separation, platform policies, identity signals simultaneously, without institutional support.

**What connects all three cases (four threads)** [P1-S22]
1. Identity must be actively constructed (language, hashtags, selfies, images of home).
2. Platforms are not neutral infrastructure (suppress, moderate, reward some identities).
3. Collective performance is the strategy (signifyin' needs an audience; queer hashtags a community; Palestinian posts a shared visual vocabulary).
4. Visibility and vulnerability are the same thing.

**The mental-health thread (four dimensions)** [P1-S23]
1. Collective protection — in-group language/imagery create safe belonging but need constant maintenance and cultural labour.
2. The cost of harassment — being visible = being a target; blocking, self-censoring, reporting become daily life.
3. Platform as adversary — biased infrastructure (moderation, algorithm, shadowbanning) makes users internalise the threat of erasure.
4. Solidarity as resilience — collective identity performance buffers individual harm.

### Part 2
**Three-part framework (Context / Concept / Implication)** [P2-S4]
- Context = Digital placemaking [P2-S5]; Concept = Symbolic geographies [P2-S6]; Implication = Countervisuality [P2-S7].

**Material vs symbolic erasure** [P2-S2] — see section 2.

**Research questions** [P2-S9]
- RQ1: Which are the themes in Palestinians' Instagram posts that contribute to the countervisual representation of Palestine?
- RQ2: In what ways do Palestinians' Instagram posts about key cities function as acts of countervisuality when compared to press coverage?
- RQ3: How does the repetition of countervisual themes relate to patterns of user engagement (likes, comments)?
- Slide 8 also states the guiding question: how do Palestinian Instagram users deploy visual symbols to construct symbolic geographies? [P2-S8]

**Method pipeline (in order)** [P2-S10 to S14, S30]
1. Identify users — 834 unique Palestinian Instagram users who publicly responded to a story template (via hareth_palestine) with their hometown names [P2-S10].
2. Data download — public posts from the first six months of the Israeli offensive, beginning October 2023 [P2-S11].
3. Location-based filtering — keep posts referencing place names within Palestine in hashtags or captions [P2-S12].
4. Final dataset — 9,126 posts from individual users [P2-S13].
5. Pilot annotation — 50 randomly selected posts to develop an initial codebook [P2-S14, S15].
6. Extended methods — mixed-method (qualitative thematic analysis + quantitative engagement metrics and statistical tests); comparative analysis vs press coverage of 5 cities (Gaza, Jerusalem, Bethlehem, Nablus, Ramallah), March 2024, from NewsAPI.org; 7,449 headlines coded with ChatGPT-4o, validated against a manual subset; chi-square tests for theme-distribution differences; composite engagement score (likes + comments); Instagram "Overperforming Score" for normalised comparison; associations of themes with engagement levels (low/med/high) [P2-S30].

**Three key visual themes (how countervisuality is expressed)** [P2-S18]
1. Everyday routines and familiar spaces to assert belonging.
2. Resilience through motifs of joy, family, and faith.
3. Cultivating knowledge through displaying places and traditions.

**Three communicative goals (how countervisuality is communicated)** [P2-S19]
1. **Claiming space** [P2-S20-S24] — visual-textual pairings asserting spatial belonging; affirming life through everyday acts (play, care, routine); possessive naming and geotagging. Symbolic imagery: *Rubble* transformed into a site of play, art, memory, symbols of life/presence/resilience/resistance [P2-S21, S22]; *The Sea* as symbol of hope, longing, freedom, resisting narratives of confinement and control [P2-S23]. Textual strategy: possessive naming/geotagging vs cartographic erasure (#pieceofpalestine, #humansofjerusalem) [P2-S24].
2. **Coding resistance** [P2-S25, S26] — strategic ambiguity (symbolism, coded language to circumvent moderation, meanings requiring cultural/contextual knowledge); recurring motifs as collective visual shorthand.
3. **Collective remembering** [P2-S27, S28] — temporal anchoring (dates, anniversaries, recurring references); repeated visual motifs reinforce shared memory; accumulating posts into collective visual archive; textual: referencing historical figures, events, place names as evidence of Palestinian presence.

## 6. Arguments, critiques and open questions
- **Anti-utopian thesis:** the "great equaliser" promise is false; visibility is unequal and platforms shape who is seen "on whose terms" [P1-S3, S22].
- **Protection/exposure paradox (Florini):** cultural competence protects; public hashtags/trending that create solidarity are the same mechanism of exposure. Power depends on staying partially opaque [P1-S11, S12].
- **Bonilla-Silva vs colourblindness:** not naming/performing identity would erase the axis of division and block struggle; performance is political, not vanity [P1-S12 notes].
- **Visibility is labour (Duguay):** rewards unequal, tied to normative femininity, mainstream celebrity, heterosexual aesthetics; costs (blocking, platform-hopping, multiple accounts) are unpaid and without institutional support [P1-S18, S19].
- **Inclusion on others' terms:** lesbian chic and gaystreaming show visibility conditional on heterosexual/white/upper-class norms [P1-S15].
- **Platform as adversary:** moderation flagged a less-exposed queer photo; algorithm/shadowbanning make users internalise threat of erasure [P1-S18, S23].
- **Erasure is both material and symbolic;** the same digital mechanisms can be "reversed, reengineered, reclaimed" [P2-S2, S3].
- **Countervisuality as non-confrontational resistance** (digital placemaking) vs algorithmic erasure and hate [P2-S7].
- **Engagement is not the goal:** resilience/solidarity themes lack strong engagement and repetition alone does not drive reach, yet repetition builds collective memory/archive (Q22) [P2-S16, S27].
- **Representation gap:** lived experience more visible on Instagram, marginal in press [P2-S17]; research gap = citizen-led visual journalism under-studied [P2-S8].
- **Limitations implicit in design (quiz Q23):** sample = users who responded to a hometown template, public posts, first six months, place-name filtered; cannot be generalised to "all Palestinians" or Instagram in general. Press comparison covers one month (March 2024) and 5 cities; coding via ChatGPT-4o validated on a manual subset [P2-S10-S13, S30].
- **Open questions** (not on slides): no outcome measures of psychological wellbeing are shown; mental-health claims are interpretive framing [P1-S19, S23].

## 7. Distractor traps
- **Reversed offline/online (Q2):** option C swaps offline/online; correct E: offline body signals automatically, online identity must be performed.
- **Right concept, wrong label (Q4, Q15, Q16):** reflexivity (feedback loop) vs refraction (unintended audiences) vs reflectivity (mirror self/hashtag declaration) vs aspirational labour (uneven capital). Match the behaviour, not the sound-alike word.
- **Swapped pairings (Q7, Q10):** hashtags paired with the wrong function (DrakePunchlines = identity signal; BlackNerdsUnite = social critique; NextOnNightline = political resistance); costs paired with the wrong example (moderation = flagged photo; self-censorship = Tumblr move; harassment = sexualising DMs).
- **Timeline mix-ups (Q9):** gaystreaming/absence/Instagram options are true statements about other eras; 1990s = lesbian chic.
- **Reversed findings (Q11, Q12, Q22, Q35):** engagement associated with protest mobilisation and everyday life, NOT resilience/solidarity; Instagram = everyday life/mobilisation/resilience, press = knowledge framing/death/destruction. "Repetition drives reach" is false; "repetition achieves nothing" is overcorrection; answer = repetition alone does not drive reach but builds archive.
- **Absolute / one-sided claims (Q19, Q20, Q21):** "purely good", "no costs", "level playing field", "everyone equal". Right answers are qualified: virality = exposure + solidarity; Instagram removes gatekeepers but adds constraints.
- **Wrong cost for the wrong community (Q14):** meaning leaks = Black Twitter; algorithmic suppression/epistemic erasure = Palestinian; self-censorship/harassment = queer women.
- **Overgeneralisation (Q23):** 9,126 posts do not mean "all Palestinians"; sample limits (834 users, template, first 6 months, place-name filter).
- **Invented method details (Q31, Q32):** correct order: users -> download set period -> place-name filter -> 50-post pilot codebook; comparison needs same places/period, shared themes, chi-square. Options with "headlines first", "different years", or "likes as measure of news" are wrong.
- **Plausible-sounding but opposite solutions (Q29, Q30, Q33, Q34, Q36, Q39):** keep code context-dependent (not explain, trend or abandon it); provide audience-separation/bulk-block tools and audit moderation (not self-censor, delete hashtags, one account); hide or abandon visibility advice ignores the visibility/vulnerability paradox.
- **Layer confusion in the Context/Concept/Implication frame (Q6, Q28):** placemaking = context, symbolic geographies = concept, countervisuality = implication; material erasure is physical, symbolic erasure is visibility/memory/narrative.
- **Who acts (Q27):** countervisuality = ordinary users from below, not platforms, press, governments or researchers.
- **Distractors with extra invented specifics:** e.g. "over several months" (Q4A), "random sample" (Q23E), "private to one friend group" (Q8B); the slides do not support them.

## 9. 60-second recap
- Promise: anyone could speak/be seen/belong; reality: visibility never neutral, belonging costs [P1-S3].
- Three cases: Black Twitter (signifyin', cost = meaning leaks), queer women on Instagram (microcelebrity, cost = harassment/moderation/self-censorship), Palestinians (countervisuality, cost = suppression/removal/epistemic erasure) [P1-S5].
- Offline the body signals race; online identity must be performed [P1-S8].
- Signifyin' = wordplay/indirection/doubleness; needs cultural competence; solidarity via collaboration; trending/virality exposes it [P1-S9, S11].
- Hashtags: DrakePunchlines = identity; BlackNerdsUnite = social critique; NextOnNightline = political resistance [P1-S10].
- Duguay: ghost effect -> lesbian chic (1990s) -> gaystreaming (2000s+) -> Instagram with new constraints [P1-S15]; reflectivity / reflexivity / refraction / aspirational labour [P1-S16].
- Costs: harassment, moderation, self-censorship, invisible labour [P1-S18]; visibility = vulnerability [P1-S22].
- Palestinian framework: placemaking (context) -> symbolic geographies (concept) -> countervisuality (implication, Mirzoeff) [P2-S4-S7].
- Method: 834 users -> Oct 2023 +6 months -> place-name filter -> 9,126 posts -> 50-post pilot; press: 5 cities, March 2024, 7,449 headlines, chi-square [P2-S10-S14, S30].
- Results: engagement tied to protest mobilisation + everyday life (not resilience/solidarity); Instagram = everyday/mobilisation/resilience vs press = knowledge framing/death/destruction [P2-S16, S17].
- Three goals: claiming space (rubble, sea, geotags), coding resistance (ambiguity, motifs), collective remembering (dates, archive) [P2-S19-S28].
- Mental-health thread: collective protection, harassment cost, platform as adversary, solidarity as resilience [P1-S23].
