## 1. One-paragraph summary

The social media landscape has shifted to algorithm-driven, infinitely scrolling, notification-heavy short-video feeds, so young people no longer search for content; content finds them [S5]. Three cases (Amy: appearance comparison and validation seeking; Brian: FoMO and problematic use; Celine: AI-generated deepfake nude) frame the question, which is analysed with a bio-psycho-social x 4Ps (predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating, protective) lens [S7-10]. International literature (Idelji-Tehrani, Messena & Everri, Popat & Tarrant, Giedinghagen, Feldman) shows both harms (comparison, pressure to stay connected, bullying, illness-content contagion) and benefits (peer support, self-expression, guided mindfulness) [S13-20]. Local data (NYMHS; Vaingankar et al.) show about 1 in 4 youths use social media excessively (>3 h/day) and about 1 in 5 were cyberbullied, each associated with roughly 1.3-2x higher odds of severe symptoms; qualitative work maps routes to positive mental health [S22-28]. Social media is not always harmful: impact depends on who uses it, why, and how [S33]. Risk and protective factors guide prevention across individual, parent, school and healthcare levels [S34-36]. Clinicians use the Idelji-Tehrani Digital Use Assessment Tool (open questions, then impact, then domain probes) and integrate digital use into safety and care plans with psychoeducation, rating scales, self-help apps and government resources (mindline.sg) [S37-52]. Emerging trends (AI, filters, influencers, mental health content) are flagged to watch [S6, S53].

## 2. Key concepts and definitions

- **Psychological well-being** — the extent to which an individual can live meaningfully according to their deeply held values (Waterman et al., 2010) [S4]. Contrast: WHO mental-health wording ("flourish, realise potential") is a distractor for this item (Q1).
- **Mental health** — a state of well-being in which an individual can flourish, through realisation of one's potential, positive social interaction, and contribution to society (WHO, 2018) [S4].
- **Evolving landscape** — algorithm-driven feeds, infinite scrolling, notifications, short-form videos, recommendation systems. Key message: "Young people are no longer actively searching for content; content increasingly finds them" [S5].
- **Bio-psycho-social + 4Ps** — Bio / Psycho / Social crossed with Predisposing / Precipitating / Perpetuating / Protective; shown as "Why the relevance?" and recapped [S10, S30]. (No elaboration on text of slides.)
- **Resilience** — a person's ability to adapt to and manage challenging and stressful events [S14].
- **Digital resilience, key factors** — ability to self-regulate Internet use; parental guidance and encouragement of safe internet use, rather than restriction; incorporate digital use into safety and care plans (Idelji-Tehrani et al., 2023) [S14].
- **Digital Technology Negative Use (DTNU)** — two conceptualisations: (1) excessive use and addiction; (2) negative online experiences or behaviours (Messena & Everri, 2023) [S15]. Well-being split into psychological, social, physical.
- **SMAAIB (Social Media Associated Abnormal Illness Behaviour)** — adolescents presenting en masse, online and in clinics, with symptoms seemingly acquired from viewing illness-related content posted by influencers (Giedinghagen) [S19]. Explanatory models in the title: mass social media induced illness; Munchausen's by internet.
- **Excessive social media use (NYMHS)** — more than three hours on social media platforms daily [S22]. It is NOT "addiction" (Q28).
- **Cyberbullying (NYMHS)** — encountering threats, harassment, or mean, aggressive or rude messages online [S23].
- **Five mechanisms by which social media affects mental health** (S32 diagram): social comparison; fear of missing out (FoMO); validation seeking; cyberbullying; problematic social media use. Slide-1 notes: "Five themes ... describing the mechanisms through which social media impacts on mental health".
- **Problematic social media use** — S32 lists loss of control, excessive use, functional impairment (Brian's case) [S8, S32].
- **Social comparison vs validation seeking vs FoMO** — comparing self with others' posts (Amy) vs deleting low-like photos and checking view counts (Amy) vs feeling you are missing something when the phone is put down (Brian) [S7, S8, S32].
- **Benefits** — social connectedness; peer support; mental health literacy/awareness; identity exploration; creativity and self-expression [S33].
- **Key message** — impact depends on who is using it, why they are using it, how they engage with it [S33].
- **Digital citizenship** — school strategy [S36]; distinct from healthcare "screening", which is a clinician role (Q23).
- **Passive scrolling** — to be reduced (individual strategy) [S35].

### 3.4 Domains of Digital Use Assessment (Idelji-Tehrani et al., 2023) [S13]
Suicide and self-harm · Substance misuse · Aggression, hate, and radicalization · Sexual risks · Gaming and gambling · Health and functioning · Cyber-crime · Digital exclusion (8 domains, in slide order).

### 3.5 Digital Use Assessment Tool — structure of questions [S37-48]
Order on slides (sections and what each asks):

1. **Opening Questions [S37]** — "Tell me a bit about your online life": What do you enjoy doing online? Do you use social media? What other online platforms do you like? What apps do you use? Do you enjoy gaming? How much time do you spend online per day?
2. **Impact on Health and Well-being [S38]** — has tech use impacted mood; self-esteem; FOMO (and how it affects you); ever lied online to improve social appearance; stopped you doing other activities; impact on sleep.
3. **Further Probes to Consider [S39]** — has anyone commented on your tech use; know anyone who made money online; do you make money online; how do you spend money online; ever lost money online (how, how much); has anyone shared something 'private' about you without consent; ever used the dark web.
4. **Aggression, Hate, and Radicalization [S40]** — see stressful or hateful things online; ever bullied online; ever thought you might have bullied someone; ever agreed with/shared views others thought extreme or dangerous; anyone knowingly shared content possibly harmful to others.
5. **Misinformation [S41]** — what online sources do you trust; do you fact check; how do you work out what is fact or fake.
6. **Digital Skills and Resilience [S42]** — offline activities you enjoy (do more?); what do you do when you see something hateful or stressful; what helps you recover; how do you avoid upsetting content; thought about how to get support if something uncomfortable happens; go online for support when upset.
7. **Suicide and Self-Harm [S43]** — see self-harm content (purposeful search or 'suggested for you' by site/app?); read/watch people with self-harm or suicidal thoughts; look for materials to help manage such thoughts; used internet to look for pro-suicide materials; researched lethal methods / tried to find or buy products to self-harm.
8. **Substance Misuse [S44]** — bought illegal drugs online; sold illegal drugs online; which platforms (e.g. social media, dark web).
9. **Gaming and Gambling [S45]** — how long do you game; does gaming get in the way of daily activities; spend money in game (skins, loot boxes); watch others gamble online; use online casinos, how much.
10. **Sexual Risks [S46]** — apps used for relationships; met anyone face to face from the internet; message people your own age; dealt with an adult interested in you; sent sexts / received unwanted sexts; sent 'nudes' / received unwanted nudes / anyone sent nudes of you; exposed to unwanted sexual content; asked to engage in sexual activity online.
11. **Cyber-crime [S47]** — victim of hacking; asked to pay a ransom; victim of fraud or identity theft; in trouble with police over online activity; uncertain about legality of online activities.
12. **Body Image [S48]** — images/influencers made you feel less good about your body; seen pro-anorexia content; purposefully searched for pro-anorexia content.

### 3.7 Risk and protective factors [S34]
Higher-risk groups: adolescents with anxiety or depression; low self-esteem; loneliness; perfectionism; previous trauma. Protective: digital literacy; strong family relationships; offline friendships; healthy coping skills; balanced online habits.

### 3.8 Promoting healthy digital well-being [S35-36]
- Individual: curate your feed; reduce passive scrolling; set screen-time boundaries; prioritise sleep.
- Parents: open conversations; role modelling; collaborative rule-setting.
- Schools: digital citizenship; cyberbullying prevention; mental health education.
- Healthcare professionals: ask about online habits during assessments; screen for cyberbullying, sleep problems, and problematic use.

### 3.9 Emerging trends [S6, S53]
- Artificial intelligence: AI-generated influencers, deepfakes, chatbots.
- Appearance filters: unrealistic beauty standards, cosmetic procedure interest.
- Influencer culture: parasocial relationships, consumerism, financial aspirations.
- Mental health content: pro = increased awareness; cons = self-diagnosis, misinformation, romanticising illness.

## 6. Arguments, critiques and open questions

- Social media is not always harmful: benefits exist (connectedness, peer support, literacy, identity, creativity) and impact depends on who/why/how [S33].
- Mixed valence within one theme: self-expression and validation can raise self-esteem/self-acceptance or trigger comparison and fear of judgement [S17].
- Association not causation: NYMHS ratios (1.3-1.6x; ~2x) are associations; direction (does distress drive use?) not established [S22-23; Q29].
- "Excessive" (>3 h) is a time-based threshold, not a clinical addiction diagnosis; Messena & Everri separate excessive use/addiction from negative online experiences [S15, S22].
- Restriction vs guidance: digital resilience favours parental guidance and encouragement over restriction [S14].
- Phones are not inherently bad: guided mindfulness apps outperform unstructured scrolling for relaxation, so repurpose rather than avoid [S20].
- Content now finds young people (algorithmic exposure) -> weakens "just choose to avoid" framing [S5].
- Mental health content: awareness (pro) vs self-diagnosis, misinformation, romanticising illness (cons); SMAAIB as extreme example [S6, S19].
- Emerging trends and AI governance ("Innovation without governance?") remain open [S53, S58].
- Discussion questions: value of self-help apps; online communities/support groups; how to build digital resilience in young people [S55-57].
- Clinicians must ask (not assume): non-judgemental open-first questioning; include digital use in safety and care plans [S36, S37, S49].

## 7. Distractor traps

- **Reversed direction / swapped lists** (Q8, Q36): risk factors (loneliness, perfectionism, trauma, anxiety, low self-esteem) placed as protective, or vice versa. Protective = digital literacy, family, offline friends, coping, balanced habits.
- **One substituted item in a list of five** (Q5, Q6, Q7): plausible but absent items (loss of privacy, online gaming, better academic results, financial independence, improved sleep).
- **Number out of context** (Q3, Q28): right 27% but wrong threshold (2 h/5 h) or wrong construct (27% = cyberbullied/addicted/severe).
- **Excessive use != addiction** (Q28): >3 h/day is a usage threshold.
- **Correlation framed as causation** (Q10, Q29): "caused", "shows that"; ratio >1 = association.
- **Largest ratio** (Q10): stress 1.6 > depression 1.5 > anxiety 1.3; "anxiety largest" is wrong.
- **Ratio misread as proportion** (Q10): "1.5 in 10" is not "1.5 times more likely".
- **Age/gender mix-ups** (Q9, Q11, Q31): excessive use higher in <30 and female; cyberbullying similar across ages and genders. Do not generalise one finding to the other.
- **Absolute claims** (Q27, Q32, Q33): "no benefits", "always better", "fully supported". Slides are nuanced; correct answers hedge or say "one-sided".
- **Restriction over guidance** (Q4, Q30): restriction, filters, monitoring, school bans are plausible but not the slide's key factor.
- **Wrong domain / wrong construct** (Q17-21): Amy = comparison + validation seeking (not problematic use); Brian = FoMO + problematic use (not validation seeking); Celine = AI deepfakes + sexual risks (not body image).
- **Wrong stakeholder** (Q22, Q23): screening is a healthcare-professional role; digital citizenship / cyberbullying prevention / mental health education are school roles; role modelling / collaborative rule-setting / open conversations are parent roles.
- **Right finding, wrong study** (Q35): peer support is positive in Popat & Tarrant and Vaingankar, not a cyber-crime domain, not Giedinghagen, not NYMHS.
- **Positive vs negative theme classification** (Q13): only "Self-expression and validation" is both; theme 4 is positive only.

## 9. 60-second recap

- Content finds young people now (algorithm feeds, infinite scroll, short video) [S5].
- Well-being (Waterman) = living meaningfully by deeply held values; mental health (WHO) = flourishing [S4].
- Five mechanisms: social comparison, FoMO, validation seeking, cyberbullying, problematic use [S32].
- Five benefits: connectedness, peer support, MH literacy, identity exploration, creativity/self-expression; impact depends on who, why, how [S33].
- NYMHS: excessive = >3 h/day, 27% (~1 in 4); <30 and female more likely; 1.5/1.3/1.6x for depression/anxiety/stress; association only [S22].
- NYMHS cyberbullying: 21% (~1 in 5); similar by age and gender; ~2x severe symptoms [S23].
- Popat & Tarrant: 24 papers, 5 themes; "Self-expression and validation" = both; appearance comparison, pressure to stay connected, bullying/harmful content = negative; peer support = positive [S16-18].
- Digital resilience: self-regulation + parental guidance (not restriction) + digital use in care plans [S14].
- Feldman: guided mindfulness beat unstructured phone use; repurpose phones, don't ban [S20].
- Giedinghagen: SMAAIB = illness symptoms acquired from influencer content [S19].
- Strategies: individual (feed, passive scrolling, boundaries, sleep); parents (talk, model, co-set rules); schools (digital citizenship, anti-cyberbullying, MH education); clinicians (ask, screen) [S35-36].
- Assessment tool order: opening -> impact on health/well-being -> further probes -> domains (aggression, misinformation, resilience, self-harm, substances, gaming, sexual, cyber-crime, body image) [S37-48]; mindline.sg for "down but unsure why" [S51].
