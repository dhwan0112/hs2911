# Demo decks for class

These decks were good because: the group uses its evidence (actual numbers, not just the direction of findings), and the deck ties the case study, the main RQ and the sub-RQs together. The best ones gave a good QnA and they had an opinion (that hopefully reflected in the concluding slides) something to share about what they learned or found hard, because they were personally invested. 
Everyone does background and concepts well enough, so that is often not a winning factor. Links to the readings of the lectures, proper links not just lip service, are appreciated a lot. 

Keep backup slides and stuff for QnA.. practice with AI (generate questions per rubric dimension) to gauge what kind of questions you are likely to get. Make sure you know your material well.

I am not sharing the decks themselves but providing a detailed description below of what they did well and what could have been better. 


## 1. TD3 Group 3: short-video addiction (the main one)

The case study is reported with its numbers (40.2% of users classed as addictive; moderate users no different from non-users on mental health), and slide 11 is the model for the whole cohort: it lists the limitations of the case study and maps each one onto the main RQ or a sub-RQ. Every sub-RQ then closes with a "link to main RQ" slide (20-21, 30, 35), slide 29 connects Sub-RQ 1 and Sub-RQ 2, and the summary (36) says what each sub-RQ added. In the Q&A they could name the scale (SAS-SV adapted for short video) and admitted straight away that nearly all their sources are from China.

Problems: the sub-RQ sections lean on theory and have few numbers of their own; slides 27-28 (Twenge) drift into general social media effects rather than short video; 

## 2. TD1 Group 1: fitspiration

The case study (Feijoo et al., 2024, N = 1,055 Spanish adolescents) is described properly: stratified sampling (slide 11), the actual survey items (12), and four findings each drawn as a path diagram (13-16), including the counter-intuitive one (body image concerns raise health-based exercise motivation) and the null result (age doesn't moderate). Each sub-RQ opens with "gaps in case study to be addressed", re-shows the relevant path diagram, then extends it. Sub-RQ 3 (slide 38) questions the case study's own gender finding (males under-report body dissatisfaction, Jankowski et al., 2018), which is exactly the critical reading we want. The main RQ is answered with a 28-day intensive longitudinal study (Fioravanti et al., 2023) rather than a blog claim.

Problems: the case study is presented as directions and diagrams, with no coefficients; Sub-RQ 1 (motivations) is a list without numbers; a Medium article is the source for algorithmic curation.

## 3. TD5 Group 2: sadfishing

The best of the sample on evidence. The case study (Shabahang et al., 2023, N = 345 Iranian adolescents) is reported with its betas and p-values (slides 7-8), and the group then criticises it: perceived social support isn't received support (Lakey & Cassady, 1990; Wethington & Kessler, 1986), and the literature is all individual-level. The main RQ comes out of that criticism (slide 15), Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems organises the three sub-RQs, and slide 20 shows the levels they dropped and why. Sub-RQ 2 has concrete numbers (negative content gets 12% more engagement across 30M tweets), Sub-RQ 3 brings in culture (Liu, 2025; the #打工人 trend). The conclusion (43) checks off: answered the main RQ, addressed the literature gap, addressed the received-support criticism. Visibility is linked to the Week 2 reading.

Problems: the "temporal variability" argument on slide 13 rests on a magazine article; a few sources are thin (a blog, a master's thesis); slide 32 stacks four citations after two numbers, so it isn't clear which supports which; the claim that environment matters more than individual traits is asserted more than shown.

## 4. TD5 Group 3: romanticisation and self-diagnosis

Densest on evidence. Case study numbers on slides 12-14 (1 in 4 self-diagnosed via social media; 30% of Gen Z; 43% sought professional confirmation; 82% confirmed). Sub-RQ 2 has accuracy rates for autism and ADHD videos (27% accurate; 52% misleading; Monteith et al., 2024), a Cochrane review (22 studies, 4,490 participants) and verbatim YouTube comments from Ng & Tandoc (2020). The conclusion (46) comes back to the case study figure.

Problems: the case study is an industry survey (Tebra, a medical software company), not a research paper. The Shah (2010) link between internet use and suicide rates (slide 29) is an ecological correlation and shouldn't be presented as a risk of self-diagnosis. The main RQ is answered conceptually (two mechanisms, slides 18-19) rather than with data.

## Other common pitfalls

- (self-diagnosis): case study is a CNN article plus a bachelor's thesis; pie chart with no source; the limitations slide is lifted from a different paper (Andalibi et al. on Instagram #depression).
- (diet trends): the main RQ is worded differently on slides 16 and 17; a 1983 paper is cited for #fitspiration and #thinspiration. The personal FYP screenshot (slide 31) is a nice touch though.
- (anonymity): reports a null moderation result - but then why keep it as a subrq? can report it honestly elsewhere or in limitations/criticisms


## The fifteen minutes: what matters and how to budget it

A question from the groups: how to focus on the important things, what the important things are, and how to split fifteen minutes. The decks above are the answer in practice, so the budget below is drawn from them.

The important things are the ones the presentation is being evaluated on: the case study's actual findings, the gap in it that motivates each sub-RQ, the evidence that answers each sub-RQ, the link back to the main RQ, and the limits of what the evidence can say. Everything else is scaffolding.

| Part | Time | What goes in |
|---|---|---|
| Case study | 3 min | What it did (sample, design, how the key variable was measured), the two or three numbers that matter, and what it cannot tell us. TD3 Group 3 and TD1 Group 1 did this well. The limitations slide is the hinge of the whole presentation, because the sub-RQs come out of it. |
| Main RQ and sub-RQs | 1 min | State them once, show how they map onto the gaps (TD3 Group 3 and TD1 Group 1). No need to re-read them at every section break. |
| Sub-RQ 1, 2, 3 | 3 min each | One or two sources per sub-RQ, with their numbers (sample, effect, direction), a line on what the sources can't show, and one sentence linking back to the main RQ. A theory earns its place only if it explains a finding; four theories where one would do is where most of the time goes. |
| Putting it together | 2 min | Answer the main RQ in one sentence, say what each sub-RQ added (TD3 Group 3; TD5 Group 2), flag the limits (cross-sectional, one country, one platform) and one future direction. Keep the language associational. |

That is fifteen minutes with no background section. Background gets folded into the first minute of the case study: one sentence on why the topic matters, ideally one number, then straight into the paper. The things that don't need airtime, with examples from the sample:

- A history of the platform 
- Definitions the audience already has (keep it in the slide but can just show and move over it).
- Lists of interventions with no evidence behind them, unless you point at one and use it to motivate the next rq, in which case treat them as evidence and report what was tested.
- Anything that doesn't connect to the main RQ
